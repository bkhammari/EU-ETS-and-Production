\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU~ETS), operational since 2005, raises production costs for emission-intensive manufacturers through a cap on aggregate emissions and a market-determined permit price. Standard trade theory predicts that an asymmetric carbon cost shifts comparative advantage toward jurisdictions with weaker regulation, generating \emph{carbon leakage}: an increase in foreign emissions that partially offsets domestic abatement \citep{fowlie2016}. The incentive is strongest for homogeneous commodities traded at common world prices, where regulated producers cannot pass the carbon cost on to buyers and imports substitute for domestic output at the margin. Whether this mechanism operates through trade flows, as opposed to physical relocation, remains an open empirical question. Aggregate studies of EU manufacturing trade find no statistically significant leakage effect \citep{naegele2019}, yet aggregate estimates may mask leakage concentrated in specific bilateral corridors where regulatory asymmetry is large, transport costs are low, and production capacity in emission-intensive sectors already exists.

This paper proposes and defends an empirical design that would estimate the differential semi-elasticity of EU imports of emission-intensive goods from North African partners (Morocco, Algeria, Egypt, Tunisia) with respect to the effective EU~ETS carbon cost over 2005 to 2021. A positive coefficient would be consistent with the import-leakage channel operating in this corridor; it would not by itself establish physical relocation of production. North Africa is a natural candidate for corridor-specific leakage: the four partners lie within the Euro-Mediterranean trade area, they host cement, fertiliser, and steel capacity oriented toward EU demand, and they face no comparable carbon-pricing regime. These are precisely the sectors and product categories covered by the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM; Regulation (EU) 2023/956), which began its transitional phase in October 2023 \citep{cbam2023}. The proposed sample window ends in 2021, before the CBAM proposal could plausibly affect trade patterns, so the design would quantify the pre-CBAM baseline that the mechanism is intended to correct.

The identification strategy rests on a triple-difference specification. Treatment intensity varies across products through their carbon-cost exposure, measured as the CO$_2$ emission intensity of the supplying sector. Treatment varies over time through the effective carbon price, defined as the annual European Union Allowance (EUA) price attenuated by the sector-year free-allocation share. Geographic variation distinguishes the North African corridor from a comparison partner group. Product-by-year fixed effects absorb global product-specific demand and supply shocks, such as Chinese steel overcapacity or commodity-price movements, that are correlated with emission intensity. Partner-by-year fixed effects absorb macroeconomic conditions, exchange-rate fluctuations, and all time-varying partner-level confounds. The triple interaction is therefore identified from the differential response of high-exposure products sourced specifically from lower-regulation partners, net of both product-level and partner-level time-varying confounds.

The paper contributes to the empirical literature on carbon leakage in three respects. First, it shifts the unit of analysis from EU-wide averages to a geographically targeted corridor where leakage incentives are strongest, directly addressing the aggregation concern raised by \citet{verde2020}. Second, it incorporates free-allocation attenuation into the treatment definition rather than treating it as a robustness dimension, reflecting the policy design that explicitly used free allocation to mitigate leakage risk \citep{martin2016}. Third, it proposes a pre-CBAM baseline estimate against which the mechanism's effectiveness could eventually be assessed.
