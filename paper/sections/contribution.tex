\section{Contribution and Use to Other Researchers}
\label{sec:contribution}

The proposed design would contribute along three dimensions. First, geographic targeting: existing trade-flow evidence pools all EU partners, so corridor-specific leakage is diluted. By restricting the analysis to North Africa, the design isolates a corridor where regulatory asymmetry is large, transport costs are low, and production capacity in emission-intensive sectors already exists. A positive $\beta$ would indicate that the aggregate nulls documented by \citet{naegele2019} conceal heterogeneity, a finding directly relevant for calibrating CBAM border adjustments by partner country. It would also supply an empirical corridor-level benchmark for the leakage responses that ex-ante evaluations of the CBAM currently derive from calibrated simulation models \citep{bellora2023}. A well-identified null would carry a different but equally informative implication: that free allocation has been sufficient to prevent trade diversion even where it is most likely.

Second, treatment measurement: by defining the treatment as $C_{st} = P_t \times (1 - a_{st})$, the design measures the carbon cost net of the allocation subsidy, rather than using a binary EU~ETS coverage indicator or the nominal EUA price. This distinction matters: during most of the sample, sectors on the carbon-leakage list received 100 per cent of the benchmark allocation for free, so their effective carbon cost was near zero despite a positive permit price. Firm-level studies that treat the nominal EUA price, or a binary coverage indicator, as the treatment overstate cost exposure for these sectors and understate the true treatment contrast across sectors. Researchers estimating competitiveness, innovation, or emissions effects in other contexts would benefit from the same adjustment.

Third, temporal coverage: most existing studies end before 2015, so the post-2018 price acceleration, which produced the strongest effective carbon cost in the system's history, remains untested for leakage.

The triple-difference structure is adaptable to other bilateral trade questions where regulation differentially affects product categories and partners. The comparison-group choice, the free-allocation treatment, and the absorption logic are transferable to studies of CBAM effectiveness once the definitive regime generates post-treatment variation.
